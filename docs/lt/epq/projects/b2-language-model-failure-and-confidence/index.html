Can a small language model tell you when it is likely to be wrong?

Project overview

You could investigate whether a language model’s own probabilities can warn you when its answer is unreliable. A model can sound confident and be wrong. You would then test whether that confidence is measurable, and whether it tracks accuracy. You would run BritLLM, the 3B model you would run locally, on a set of questions with known answers. For each question you would score the model’s probability of the correct answer and of the answer it prefers. Then you would check whether high confidence means high accuracy.

This would be a project about calibration: the relationship between the confidence a model expresses and the proportion of times it is right. You would build reliability diagrams, measure expected calibration error, and test a practical use of confidence: selective prediction. You would test how much accuracy the model gains, and how many questions it has to give up, if it answers only when it is confident. You could compare 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit models, and compare different kinds of question.

You would measure confidence from the model’s probabilities, not from how confident the text sounds. This is more precise than counting hedging words, and it lets you test whether the model “knows what it knows” in a way that can be checked. You could also compare probability confidence with the model’s verbal confidence, if you can elicit a number from it.

Your final artefact could be a Python harness, a question set you designed, calibration plots for each model, and a written analysis of when confidence is useful and when it is misleading.

Dataset(s) to be used

The models are the local BritLLM MLX quantisations: 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit. The model card records the licence and context length. The model has 3B parameters and a 2048-token context. All three quantisations run on a 16 GB M-series Mac. Start with one quantisation and add the others once the harness works.

Your main dataset is a question set you write yourself. Aim for 100–200 items. Each item needs:

  • a short prompt, such as The capital of France is
  • a correct answer, such as Paris
  • two or three plausible distractors, such as Lyon and Marseille
  • a category, such as factual recall, arithmetic, science, history, or a made-up entity
  • a difficulty rating, which you assign before you see the model’s results
  • a source for the correct answer, so that you can check it later

A candidate-based format is a good fit for a base model. The model has not been instruction-tuned, so it may continue text rather than answer a question. Asking it to choose between candidates turns the task into a probability comparison, which is exactly what you need for calibration. It also keeps the scoring automatic and reproducible.

You could include easy, medium and hard items, and include some questions about things that do not exist, such as a made-up capital city or a fictional chemical element. A model that is confident about a fictional fact is showing you something important. Include questions with ambiguous answers, and decide in advance how you would handle them; ambiguity is part of the phenomenon, but it complicates the scoring.

If you want a larger or more standard set, you could use a public multiple-choice dataset such as AI2 Reasoning Challenge (allenai/ai2_arc) or OpenBookQA (allenai/openbookqa). Check the dataset card and licence, and consider whether the model may have seen the questions during pre-training. A public benchmark is useful for comparison, but your own questions give you more control over difficulty and contamination.

For a general measure of the model’s surprise, use a held-out text: a public-domain book chapter or a Wikipedia extract. Compute perplexity on it for each quantisation. This is not a confidence measure for individual answers, but it tells you whether the model is generally more or less uncertain after compression.

If you also want to compare verbal confidence, you need prompts that ask the model to state a probability, such as How confident are you, from 0 to 100?. A base model may not follow that instruction reliably. You could treat verbal confidence as a separate, optional experiment, and say clearly that it is a different measurement.

Suggested research question or hypothesis

For example:

Can the token probabilities of a small language model be used to predict when its answers are wrong, and how well calibrated is that confidence across task types and quantisation levels?

You could develop this into more specific questions, such as whether high confidence implies high accuracy, whether 3-bit is less calibrated than 8-bit, whether the model is more overconfident on rare facts or on arithmetic, or whether selective prediction can give a useful accuracy gain at a reasonable coverage.

You could test whether the model is well calibrated on easy, common facts and overconfident on hard or rare ones. You could test that by grouping the questions by difficulty and category and plotting a separate reliability diagram for each group.

Key data science, statistical and machine learning concepts

Your project could involve:

  • probability, log-probability and the softmax function
  • scoring a fixed answer by summing the log-probabilities of its tokens
  • length normalisation and why answer length is important
  • accuracy, confidence and the difference between them
  • calibration and reliability diagrams
  • expected calibration error (ECE) and the Brier score
  • selective prediction, coverage and the risk-coverage curve
  • baselines: random choice, the most frequent answer, and always answering
  • sampling versus scoring: generating an answer versus measuring the probability of a fixed answer
  • temperature and how it changes the distribution
  • tokenisation and multi-token answers
  • question design, candidate sets and difficulty
  • experimental control across quantisations
  • confidence intervals and the uncertainty of a proportion
  • the difference between “the model is uncertain” and “the question is ambiguous”
  • contamination and benchmark leakage
  • verbal confidence and its limits

The central idea is calibration. A model is calibrated if, among all the questions where it says it is 80% confident, it is right about 80% of the time. Perfect calibration is rare. The useful question is how far from calibrated the model is, and whether the gap is stable enough to support a decision.

Suggested approaches to data preparation, analysis and modelling

You could begin by building the question set in a spreadsheet or a CSV file. You would keep the prompt, the correct answer, the distractors, the category, the difficulty and the source in separate columns. Write 20 pilot items first and run them through the model. If the model gets everything right or everything wrong, adjust the difficulty before writing the full set.

You could write a scorer in Python. For each question and each candidate answer:

  1. Build the text prompt + " " + answer.
  2. Tokenise it with the model’s tokenizer.
  3. Run the model on the token IDs and collect the logits.
  4. Sum the log-probabilities of the answer tokens, using the logits at the positions that predict each token.
  5. Divide by the number of answer tokens to length-normalise.
  6. Convert the candidate scores to probabilities with a softmax.

The result is a probability for each candidate. Record the top candidate, its probability, the margin between the top two, and whether it is correct. Check the scorer on a few examples by comparing the top candidate with a greedy generation: they should usually agree. If they do not, the prompt or the tokenisation is probably wrong.

You could run the same questions through the 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit models. Keep the prompt, the candidate set, the tokeniser and the scoring rule identical. The models share a tokenizer, so the comparison is fair. If you change the prompt format between models, you are measuring the prompt, not the quantisation.

Compute the basic statistics for each model:

  • accuracy: the proportion of questions where the top candidate is correct
  • mean confidence: the average probability of the top candidate
  • the calibration curve: bin the questions by confidence (for example, 0–10%, 10–20%, …) and plot the observed accuracy in each bin against the mean confidence
  • expected calibration error: a weighted average of the gap between confidence and accuracy across the bins
  • the Brier score: the mean squared error between the predicted probability of the chosen answer and whether it was correct

You could plot a reliability diagram for each model and each task category. A model that is overconfident sits below the diagonal: it claims more certainty than it delivers. A model that is underconfident sits above it. Look at the number of questions in each bin; a bin with three questions is not evidence of much.

Then test selective prediction. Sort the questions by confidence and answer only the top proportion. Plot accuracy against coverage. You could compare accuracy at 50% and 80% coverage, find the confidence threshold that gives 90% accuracy, and report how many questions are answered at that threshold. Compare the curve for 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit. A model that is better calibrated may give a better accuracy-coverage trade-off even if its overall accuracy is similar.

If you want to compare probability confidence with verbal confidence, run a separate experiment. Ask the model to state a confidence from 0 to 100 after each answer, parse the number, and compare it with the probability-based confidence. You could ask whether the self-report tracks accuracy, and whether it is systematically higher or lower. Treat the result with caution: the self-report is generated text, not a direct readout of the model’s internal state.

If you want to test sampling consistency, generate ten answers at temperature 0.8 for each question and measure how often they agree with the top candidate. You could test whether agreement is a better predictor of correctness than the probability of the top candidate. This is related to the idea of semantic uncertainty, but keep it simple: agreement is a count, not a probability.

You should report confidence intervals for accuracy and for ECE. With 100–200 questions, the intervals are wide, especially for subgroup analyses. A bootstrap resampling of the questions is a simple way to estimate the uncertainty without new data.

You should record your decisions in your project log: the question set, the candidate sets, the length normalisation, the prompt template, the categories, the correctness rule, and how you handled ambiguous or refused answers.

Potential limitations, risks and sources of bias

Considering the limitations of your data and model is an important part of evaluating your project. For example:

  • A language model’s token probability is not a calibrated statement of truth. It reflects the likelihood of the next token given the prompt, which is influenced by phrasing, frequency and the model’s training data.
  • A base model may not treat the candidates as options. It may prefer a continuation that is grammatically likely rather than factually correct. That is part of what you are measuring, but it limits how directly you can read the result as “knowledge”.
  • The question set is small and yours. It may be biased toward facts you know and phrasings the model handles. Public benchmarks may be contaminated.
  • The candidate set affects confidence. Adding a plausible distractor changes the softmax and can make the model look less confident without changing its knowledge.
  • Length normalisation is a choice: dividing by the number of answer tokens can favour short answers, and not dividing can favour long ones. Both should be tried and the sensitivity reported.
  • Tokenisation can split an answer into several tokens, and the boundary between prompt and answer may not align with a token, so the token IDs should be checked by hand for a few examples.
  • Quantisation can change probabilities, but it can also change the speed and the kernel, so differences should not be attributed to bits without checking the configuration.
  • Calibration on one set of questions may not transfer to another, so per-category results are more informative than a single claim that the model is “generally calibrated”.
  • ECE depends on the number and width of the bins, so fixed bins, the number of items per bin and a bootstrap estimate of uncertainty should be reported.
  • The model may have seen the public benchmark questions during pre-training, so your own questions or a held-out set are safer, and the choice should be stated.
  • Verbal confidence is generated text and may be influenced by the prompt, the model’s style, or a desire to please. It is not ground truth.
  • A model that is overconfident on your questions is not necessarily overconfident on all tasks, so the conclusion should be scoped to the model, the questions and the conditions tested.
  • If you conclude that confidence is useful for selective prediction, remember that a wrong answer that is deferred still has a cost. The coverage-accuracy curve shows the trade-off, but it does not make the decision for you.

You should consider how these limitations affect the claims you can reasonably make from your model.

Expected outcomes and opportunities for evaluation

The expected outcome would be a Python harness that scores candidate answers, a question set you designed, calibration plots and a coverage-accuracy curve for each quantisation. Importantly, a model that is poorly calibrated is not a failed project. The size and direction of the miscalibration are more important than a single accuracy figure, and so is whether it is stable enough to be useful.

You could evaluate the models on overall accuracy, calibration and selective prediction, and compare the results across task categories and bit widths. You could also compare probability confidence with verbal confidence, and ask whether the two agree. A model whose words and probabilities disagree would be worth discussing.

You could pick three questions where the model was confidently wrong and three where it was uncertain and right. You could ask what made the difference, and whether the question was rare, ambiguous, or phrased in an unusual way. If you can answer that, you have a stronger evaluation than a single calibration score.

You could finish by reflecting on what confidence would need to be useful in a real application: a reliable threshold, a clear cost for deferring, and a way to communicate uncertainty to a user.

Suitability as an EPQ artefact

This could make a strong EPQ artefact because designing the question set and the scoring method requires you to make a series of decisions that you can research, justify and evaluate. The harness provides a tangible outcome, while the analysis provides evidence of planning, research, problem-solving and reflection. The code is mostly scoring and plotting; the main work is designing the questions and interpreting the calibration.

Crucially, the project is open-ended. There is no single correct question set, confidence measure or threshold, and the quality of your EPQ does not depend simply on finding a model that is perfectly calibrated. You have scope to make and justify your own decisions about the task categories, the definition of a correct answer, and the kind of uncertainty that is most important.

You could also discuss questions from AI safety and human-computer interaction: how people interpret confidence, when a model should refuse, and why a fluent answer is not the same as a reliable one. You could use your calibration curves as evidence in that discussion.
