Can classmates tell BritLLM’s writing from a human’s when the human is imitating the machine?

Project overview

You could run your own Turing-style test with BritLLM, the 3B language model you would run locally. You would generate text on fixed prompts, write human counterparts that are deliberately styled to be confusable, and ask classmates to judge which is which. The model runs locally on your laptop, so the texts stay private and the experiment can be repeated. Your project would investigate whether people can tell its writing from human writing when the human is trying to sound machine-like, and what gives the machine away.

The human texts are the other half of the design. A plain human-versus-machine test often measures whether the human text is longer, funnier or more topical. If your human writers deliberately copy BritLLM’s habits, such as even sentence length, tidy structure and hedging phrases, you can distinguish between spotting the machine and spotting the writing style. You could also compare quantisations: whether 3-bit BritLLM sounds more or less human than 8-bit, and whether a higher temperature makes the text more surprising or just less coherent.

You could produce a set of matched human and machine texts, an anonymous judging sheet, a table of judgements, and a written analysis of the tells. Your project would be about designing a fair comparison, collecting judgements, and interpreting what people noticed.

Dataset(s) to be used

The model is BritLLM, a 3B parameter language model with a 2048-token context, released under the ODC-BY licence. The model card and local MLX quantisations are the practical starting point. The local quantisations include 3-bit, 4-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit; the main comparison in this project uses 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit. They run locally on an Apple Silicon Mac and do not need a network connection once downloaded. Start with one quantisation and add the others once your generation procedure is stable.

Your main dataset is one you create:

  • Prompts: 10–20 openings or instructions, chosen to suit your genre.
  • Machine texts: 3–5 generations per prompt from each quantisation, at fixed temperature, seed and maximum length.
  • Human texts: counterparts written by you, classmates or a public-domain author. It helps if they match the machine texts in topic, length and format, though that is a workload of its own: decide early how many human texts you need.
  • Judgements: each judge’s guess, confidence rating and short reason, under an anonymous code.

You would need to choose a genre. Short story openings allow both the model and the human writer to produce a substantial piece of text. Persuasive paragraphs test structure and rhetoric. Explanations of a school topic test factual accuracy. Limericks are short, highly structured and funny, and a public-domain collection is available: Edward Lear’s A Book of Nonsense (1846) is available from Project Gutenberg. If you use Lear, remember that the Victorian style is itself a clue, so include some modern human limericks as well — check the licence, or ask permission, before you copy one into your dataset.

For the human side, you can write your own texts, ask classmates for contributions, or use public-domain writing. If you involve other people, as contributors or as judges, consider whether you need approval from your school, and how you will keep them anonymous.

In Orange, load the BritLLM model with Load LM and connect its Model output to LM Generator. Choose the quantisation you want to test, and keep the prompt, temperature, maximum new tokens, number of generations and seed identical across models. Load LM reads the model directory directly, so you can switch between 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit without leaving the canvas.

Example research questions or hypothesis

For example:

When classmates compare human writing with text generated by BritLLM, what makes the machine detectable, and does quantisation or sampling temperature change how human it seems?

You could develop this into more specific questions, such as whether 3-bit outputs are easier to spot than 8-bit outputs, whether low or high temperature produces more human-seeming text, whether judges rely more on rhythm and vocabulary than on meaning, or whether a short guide to machine-writing tells improves their accuracy.

You could test whether judges find 3-bit text easier to spot, and whether that goes with the repetition and coherence you measure separately — measure the two rather than assuming one explains the other. You could test that by comparing detection rates for each quantisation, using the same prompts, seeds and maximum lengths.

Key data science, statistical and machine learning concepts

Your project could involve:

  • the Turing test as an experimental design as well as a philosophical idea
  • human baselines and blind judging
  • randomisation and counterbalancing the order of items
  • sample size, binomial uncertainty and confidence intervals
  • inter-rater agreement, in simple terms
  • confounding variables: length, topic, genre, spelling, punctuation and period
  • held-out human texts and avoiding contamination
  • tokenisation and how the model splits text into tokens
  • quantisation: 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit weights
  • base models versus instruction-tuned models
  • sampling temperature and the trade-off between predictability and variety
  • text analysis: word frequency, repetition, collocation, vocabulary and sentence length
  • coding free-text reasons into categories
  • the difference between “sounds human” and “is good”

The language-model widgets support the comparison. Load LM loads a local model directory. LM Generator exposes the prompt, temperature, maximum new tokens, number of generations and seed. LM Vocab Viewer shows the vocabulary and token counts. Orange3-Text adds Corpus, Word Cloud, Concordance and Document Map if you want to compare the vocabulary of human and machine texts.

Example approaches to data preparation, analysis and modelling

One comparison — a few prompts, a couple of generations each and a small panel of judges — would be enough to see what the question involves.

You could begin by choosing a genre and writing 10–20 prompts. Keep them short enough that a generation fits within the model’s context, and varied enough that you are not testing one lucky phrasing. For each prompt, generate 3–5 texts from each quantisation at a fixed temperature, seed and maximum length.

Potential limitations, risks and sources of bias

The limitations you could consider include:

  • The judge sample is small. Fifteen judges times twenty pairs is three hundred judgements, enough to estimate a proportion roughly but not to compare many subgroups, so the uncertainty should be reported and subgroup comparisons treated with caution.
  • Judges who know the project may be biased, so a standard written briefing, without explaining the hypothesis, is safer. The debrief should come after the judging is finished.
  • Order effects and fatigue are important. A judge who sees ten machine texts in a row may start expecting another one, so the order should be randomised, the session kept short, and every judge briefed identically.
  • The human texts may be unfair if they are longer, funnier or on easier topics, in which case you would be measuring that rather than machine-ness. Length, topic and format should be matched deliberately.
  • The genre may dominate the result. Limericks, story openings and explanations make different demands on a model, and a result in one genre may not transfer to another.
  • BritLLM is a base model, not an instruction-tuned assistant. It may continue text rather than answer a question, especially at low temperatures. That is a property of the model you are testing, but it shapes the prompts you can use.
  • The difference between 3-bit and 8-bit may be too small for your judges to detect. A null result is a finding, provided your sample is large enough to support it.
  • Sampling temperature and seed add variability, so they should be fixed across comparisons, and each prompt repeated several times if you want to separate model quality from sampling luck.
  • The model may have seen a public-domain human text during pre-training, and a judge who recognises a passage is testing memory, not style. Texts the model is unlikely to have memorised are safer, or the risk should be acknowledged.
  • The Turing test is a measure of discrimination in a particular setting, not a test of intelligence, so passing or failing it should not be claimed to settle a philosophical question.
  • Writing human texts that imitate the machine can make them unnatural, so the pairs should be piloted and any that are obviously written to a formula removed.

Think about how these limitations affect the claims you can reasonably make from your experiment.

Expected outcomes and opportunities for evaluation

The expected outcome would be a set of matched human and machine texts, a judging dataset, and an estimate of how accurately classmates distinguish them. A model that fails the Turing test gives you the tells to explain: which gave it away, and how reliably.

You could evaluate the overall detection rate, the detection rate for each quantisation and temperature, and the reasons judges gave. You could compare the confusion matrix for human and machine items, and ask whether judges are better at spotting 3-bit than 8-bit text. You could also compare the vocabulary and repetition of human and machine texts, and connect those measurements to the reasons judges gave.

You could pick three pairs that were judged incorrectly and read them closely. You could ask what made the human text look machine-like, or the machine text look human. Answering that takes you beyond a single detection rate. You could finish by considering what the experiment says about the Turing test as a measure: whether it tests intelligence, imitation, or the judge’s expectations, and what would change if the judges were experts, the texts were longer, or the machine were allowed to revise its output. You could discuss those questions in your evaluation.
