Can a country’s income explain how long its people live, and what do the exceptions tell us?

Project overview

You could investigate the relationship between a country’s income and the life expectancy of its people, and use the exceptions to ask what income leaves out. The starting point would be the pattern made famous by Gapminder: richer countries tend to live longer. You would build a regression model in Orange Data Mining to predict life expectancy from income per person, then examine the countries that sit far above or below the trend. You would consider which countries live much longer or much shorter than their income predicts, and what else might explain the gap: schooling, child mortality, health spending, geography, inequality or conflict.

A high R² is not necessary. A simple model will explain much of the variation, and the exceptions are what it leaves to explain. You would learn to read a scatter plot, fit and interpret a regression, inspect residuals, add a second indicator without fooling yourself, and separate correlation from causation.

You could produce the workflow, a scatter plot with the fitted line, a residual table naming the biggest exceptions, a comparison of two or three models, and a short case study of one over-performing and one under-performing country.

Dataset(s) to be used

A suitable starting point would be Gapminder, the Swedish foundation co-founded by Hans Rosling. Gapminder publishes free datasets and visualisations about global development. Its best-known chart plots income per person against life expectancy, with each country drawn as a bubble whose size is its population. Gapminder is not a single file: you download the indicators you need from Gapminder’s data page, usually as a spreadsheet or CSV, and combine them yourself.

Useful indicators include:

  • income per person (GDP per capita, adjusted for purchasing power)
  • life expectancy at birth
  • mean years of schooling
  • child mortality
  • fertility rate
  • health spending per person
  • CO₂ emissions per person

Gapminder’s data is free to use with attribution. Check the licence note on each indicator download and record the source and download date in your project log. The World Bank publishes similar indicators (life expectancy at birth, GDP per capita, health expenditure, immunisation rates) and is a useful second source. The definitions are not identical: “income per person” may use purchasing-power parity or market exchange rates, and “life expectancy” is an estimate rather than a count. Comparing sources is part of the investigation.

You would need to decide which countries and years to include. A single recent year with good coverage gives you one row per country and a clean cross-section. A panel of several decades lets you ask whether the relationship changes over time, but it makes the data preparation harder. Country borders change, some states are very small, and many indicators have missing values. Write down your inclusion rules before you start.

Income is heavily skewed: a few countries are far richer than the rest. Plotting income on a logarithmic scale usually makes the relationship closer to a straight line, and it changes which countries look like outliers. You could add a log-transformed column in a spreadsheet, or use Feature Constructor in Orange to create one. Record the choice, because it affects the model and the interpretation.

The data is aggregate national statistics, so there are no personal-data issues. There is an interpretation issue: a country average does not describe the people inside it. A country can have a high average income and a low life expectancy because of inequality, or a low average income and a high life expectancy because of strong public health. Keep that distinction visible in your writing.

Example research questions or hypothesis

For example:

To what extent can income per person explain life expectancy across countries, and what do the exceptions tell us about the limits of the relationship?

You could develop this into more specific questions, such as whether a log-transformed income fits better than raw income, which countries are the largest positive and negative residuals, whether adding schooling or child mortality improves the model, whether the pattern holds within regions and across decades, or whether the relationship is causal.

You could test whether income explains a substantial part of the variation, and whether the residuals cluster by region and by health-system quality. You could test that by colouring the scatter plot by region, fitting separate models, and comparing the residuals.

Key data science, statistical and machine learning concepts

Your project could involve:

  • scatter plots, correlation and the shape of a relationship
  • linear regression and the meaning of a coefficient
  • logarithmic scales and why income is often plotted on a log axis
  • R², root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE)
  • residuals and what a residual plot can reveal
  • outliers and influential points
  • training, validation and test data, and cross-validation
  • multiple regression and feature importance
  • confounding: a third variable that affects both income and health
  • multicollinearity: income and schooling are correlated, so their coefficients can be unstable
  • Simpson’s paradox: a relationship that changes direction when you split the data into groups
  • the ecological fallacy: country averages do not describe individuals
  • correlation versus causation
  • data definitions, missing values and changing country borders
  • extrapolation and the limits of a model outside the data it saw

Orange’s Scatter Plot can draw the relationship and a regression line. Linear Regression fits the model and shows the coefficients. Test & Score reports R², RMSE and MAE under cross-validation. Predictions shows predicted versus actual values and the residuals. Feature Statistics and Data Table help you inspect distributions and individual countries. Feature Constructor creates a log-income column, and Preprocess can standardise features if you want to compare coefficients on a common scale.

Example approaches to data preparation, analysis and modelling

You could fit the single-year model, look at the residuals, then decide whether a log transform or a regional split is worth the time.

You could begin by downloading two indicators, income per person and life expectancy, for a recent year. Put them in a spreadsheet with one row per country, check the missing values, and import the CSV into Orange with File. You could open Scatter Plot, put income on the x-axis and life expectancy on the y-axis, and colour the points by region.

Potential limitations, risks and sources of bias

The limitations you could consider include:

  • The ecological fallacy is the central risk. A country-level relationship does not tell you what happens to an individual, and a country average can hide enormous inequality.
  • Income is correlated with many other things: education, health systems, sanitation, geography, colonial history, conflict and inequality. A regression coefficient is not a causal effect.
  • Data definitions differ between Gapminder and the World Bank. Mixing sources without checking definitions can change the result.
  • Missing values are common, especially for poorer countries and earlier decades. Dropping them silently can bias the sample.
  • Country borders and names change. The USSR, Yugoslavia and other historical states need a consistent rule.
  • A few very rich or very small countries can dominate the fit, so the effect of removing the largest outliers should be checked.
  • There are only about 200 countries, so a handful of unusual cases can change the coefficients. The uncertainty should be reported and the robustness of the conclusion tested.
  • Linear regression assumes a straight-line relationship. Log income often fits better, but it is still an approximation.
  • R² does not prove causation, and a high R² does not mean the model is useful for policy. Omitted variables can bias every coefficient.
  • The data is historical and is revised, so the download date and the version of each indicator should be recorded.
  • Extrapolating beyond the observed range of income or life expectancy is unsafe, so the model should not be used to predict countries or years it has not seen.
  • Choosing the year, the countries or the exception criterion after seeing the results makes the analysis look stronger than it is, so the main choices should be recorded in the log before the analysis.

Think about how these limitations affect the claims you can reasonably make from your model.

Expected outcomes and opportunities for evaluation

The expected outcome would be a working Orange workflow that predicts life expectancy from income, a scatter plot with a fitted line, a residual table naming the largest exceptions, and a comparison of at least two model specifications. A high R² is not necessary. A model that explains a moderate share of the variation, and a clear account of what it misses, gives you something to argue.

You could evaluate the model with cross-validation, compare it with a mean-prediction baseline, inspect the residuals for patterns, and check whether the conclusions survive a change of year, region or indicator. You could compare Gapminder with the World Bank for one or two countries, and ask whether the definitions explain any disagreement.

You could pick three countries that break the rule and write a short explanation for each. You could ask what else might explain the gap — income inequality, the health system, a recent conflict, oil wealth, or a problem with the data — and check which of those you actually have evidence for. Answering that takes you beyond a single R². If you cannot, that is also a finding about the limits of the data.

You could finish by reflecting on what a model like this could and could not be used for. It may describe a broad pattern across countries without being reliable enough to guide policy for one country. Articulating that distinction is part of the project.
