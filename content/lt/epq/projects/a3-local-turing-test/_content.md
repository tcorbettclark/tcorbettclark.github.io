# Can classmates tell BritLLM's writing from a human's when the human is imitating the machine?

> One way to run this project, not the only way. Change the question, the
data, the tool or the artefact to suit what interests you: the choices, and
the reasons for them, have to be yours before your centre approves the
proposal.

## Project overview

You could run your own Turing-style test with
[BritLLM](https://huggingface.co/britllm/britllm-3b-v0.1), the 3B language model
you would run locally. You would generate text on fixed prompts, write human
counterparts that are deliberately styled to be confusable, and ask classmates
to judge which is which. The model runs locally on your laptop, so the texts
stay private and the experiment can be repeated. Your project would investigate
whether people can tell its writing from human writing when the human is trying
to sound machine-like, and what gives the machine away.

An important part of the design would be the human texts. A plain
human-versus-machine test often measures whether the human text is longer,
funnier or more topical. If your human writers deliberately copy BritLLM's
habits, such as even sentence length, tidy structure and hedging phrases, you
can distinguish between spotting the machine and spotting the writing style.
You could also compare quantisations: whether 3-bit BritLLM sounds more or less
human than 8-bit, and whether a higher temperature makes the text more
surprising or just less coherent.

Your final artefact could be a set of matched human and machine texts, an
anonymous judging sheet, a table of judgements, and a written analysis of the
tells. Your project would be about designing a fair comparison, collecting
judgements, and interpreting what people noticed.

**What you submit.** The assessed product is a written report: around 5,000
words for a research project, or at least 1,000 words alongside an artefact
such as the one described here. The tables, plots and workflow are evidence
you discuss in the report, not the submission itself.

## Dataset(s) to be used

The model is **BritLLM**, a 3B parameter language model with a 2048-token
context, released under the ODC-BY licence. The [model card](https://huggingface.co/britllm/britllm-3b-v0.1)
and local MLX quantisations are the practical starting point. The local
quantisations include 3-bit, 4-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit; the main comparison in
this project uses 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit. They run locally on an Apple Silicon
Mac and do not need a network connection once downloaded. Start with one
quantisation and add the others once your generation procedure is stable.

Your main dataset is one you create:

- **Prompts:** 10–20 openings or instructions, chosen to suit your genre.
- **Machine texts:** 3–5 generations per prompt from each quantisation, at
  fixed temperature, seed and maximum length.
- **Human texts:** counterparts written by you, classmates or a public-domain
  author. They must match the machine texts in topic, length and format.
- **Judgements:** each judge's guess, confidence rating and short reason, under
  an anonymous code.

You would need to choose a genre. Short story openings allow both the model and
the human writer to produce a substantial piece of text. Persuasive paragraphs
test structure and rhetoric. Explanations of a school topic test factual accuracy.
Limericks are short, highly structured and funny, and a public-domain collection
is easy to find: Edward Lear's *A Book of Nonsense* (1846) is available from
[Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13646). If you use Lear,
remember that the Victorian style is itself a clue, so include some modern human
limericks as well.

For the human side, you can write your own texts, ask classmates for
contributions, or use public-domain writing. If you ask classmates, get their
consent, keep the texts anonymous, and do not collect anything more personal
than the writing itself. Check whether your school requires approval for a
questionnaire or a judging session.

In Orange, load the BritLLM model with **Load LM** and connect its **Model**
output to **LM Generator**. Choose the quantisation you want to test, and keep
the prompt, temperature, maximum new tokens, number of generations and seed
identical across models. **Load LM** reads the model directory directly, so you
can switch between 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit without leaving the canvas.

## Suggested research question or hypothesis

For example:

> When classmates compare human writing with text generated by BritLLM, what
> makes the machine detectable, and does quantisation or sampling temperature
> change how human it seems?

You could develop this into more specific questions, such as whether 3-bit
outputs are easier to spot than 8-bit outputs, whether low or high temperature
produces more human-seeming text, whether judges rely more on rhythm and
vocabulary than on meaning, or whether a short guide to machine-writing tells
improves their accuracy.

You could test whether judges find 3-bit text easier to spot because it is
more repetitive and less coherent, while 8-bit text is harder. You could test
that by comparing detection rates for each quantisation, using the same
prompts, seeds and maximum lengths.

## Key data science, statistical and machine learning concepts

Your project could involve:

- the Turing test as an experimental design as well as a philosophical idea
- human baselines and blind judging
- randomisation and counterbalancing the order of items
- sample size, binomial uncertainty and confidence intervals
- inter-rater agreement, in simple terms
- confounding variables: length, topic, genre, spelling, punctuation and period
- held-out human texts and avoiding contamination
- tokenisation and how the model splits text into tokens
- quantisation: 3-bit, 6-bit and 8-bit weights
- base models versus instruction-tuned models
- sampling temperature and the trade-off between predictability and variety
- text analysis: word frequency, repetition, collocation, vocabulary and sentence
  length
- coding free-text reasons into categories
- the difference between "sounds human" and "is good"

The language-model widgets support the comparison. **Load LM** loads a local
model directory. **LM Generator** exposes the prompt, temperature, maximum new
tokens, number of generations and seed. **LM Vocab Viewer** shows the vocabulary
and token counts. **Orange3-Text** adds **Corpus**, **Word Cloud**,
**Concordance** and **Document Map** if you want to compare the vocabulary of
human and machine texts.

## Suggested approaches to data preparation, analysis and modelling

**How much to attempt.** One comparison is enough: a small prompt set, two
generations each and a dozen or so judges; more quantisations and more judges
are extensions. You need Orange with the add-on's language-model widgets and
`mlx-lm` on an Apple-silicon Mac — see [Prerequisites](../../prerequisites/).

You could begin by choosing a genre and writing 10–20 prompts. Keep them short
enough that a generation fits within the model's context, and varied enough that
you are not testing one lucky phrasing. For each prompt, generate 3–5 texts from
each quantisation at a fixed temperature, seed and maximum length. Save every
generation with its prompt, model, temperature and seed in a spreadsheet. Remove
near-duplicates of the prompt and any output that is truncated or malformed, and
note how many you removed.

You could write the human counterparts before you look at the machine outputs, or ask
someone else to write them. Match the machine texts in topic, length and format.
You would need to decide whether your human writers are trying to sound plain-human or
deliberately machine-like. The second option would let you distinguish the
ability to spot a machine from the ability to spot a style. If your human
writers are imitating BritLLM, give them a short description of its habits, but
not the exact machine texts they are paired with.

You could build the judging set: choose 20–40 pairs, balanced across quantisations and
temperatures, with one human and one machine text in each pair. Put them in a
random order, with the same formatting for both. Tell judges that both sources
are present and that guessing is allowed, but do not tell them the ratio or your
hypothesis. Present one pair at a time, and ask for a binary judgement, a
confidence rating from 1 to 5, and a short reason.

You could run a pilot with three to five judges. Look for ambiguous instructions, pairs
that are obviously broken, and items that take too long. Revise the sheet, then
run the main study with 15–30 classmates. Keep the order random for each judge
if you can; at minimum, shuffle the pairs once, use the same order for everyone,
and record that choice.

You could analyse the results in a spreadsheet or in Python. Report overall accuracy
against the 50% chance baseline, with a confidence interval. Break the accuracy
down by quantisation, by temperature, by genre and by judge. Build a confusion
matrix: how often a human text was judged machine, and a machine text judged
human. Code the free-text reasons into categories such as rhythm, vocabulary,
grammar, sense, repetition, punctuation, factual error and emotional
plausibility. Count how often each category appears for correct and incorrect
judgements.

A simple binomial calculation is enough for the uncertainty. If 18 out of 30
judgements are correct, the estimate is 60%, but the interval is wide. Report
the interval as well as the percentage, and avoid claiming a difference between
two quantisations unless the intervals are clearly separated.

If you install **Orange3-Text**, connect **LM Generator** to **Corpus** and
compare **Word Cloud** and **Concordance** for human and machine texts. You could
ask whether the machine texts overuse a small set of words, or produce
collocations that never appear in the human corpus. That evidence can support
your reading of the judges' reasons.

You should record your decisions in your project log: the genre, the prompts, the
quantisations, the temperature and seed, the human instructions, the
randomisation, and what you decided counts as "human-like".

## Potential limitations, risks and sources of bias

Considering the limitations of your data and model is an important part of
evaluating your project. For example:

- The judge sample is small. Fifteen judges times twenty pairs is three hundred
  judgements, enough to estimate a proportion roughly but not to compare many
  subgroups, so the uncertainty should be reported and subgroup comparisons
  treated with caution.
- Judges who know the project may be biased, so a standard written briefing,
  without explaining the hypothesis, is safer. The debrief should come after the
  judging is finished.
- Order effects and fatigue are important. A judge who sees ten machine texts in
  a row may start expecting another one, so the order should be randomised, the
  session kept short, and every judge briefed identically.
- The human texts may be unfair if they are longer, funnier or on easier topics,
  in which case you would be measuring that rather than machine-ness. Length,
  topic and format should be matched deliberately.
- The genre may dominate the result. Limericks, story openings and explanations
  make different demands on a model, and a result in one genre may not transfer
  to another.
- BritLLM is a base model, not an instruction-tuned assistant. It may continue
  text rather than answer a question, especially at low temperatures. That is a
  property of the model you are testing, but it shapes the prompts you can use.
- The difference between 3-bit and 8-bit may be too small for your judges to
  detect. A null result is a finding, provided your sample is large enough to
  support it.
- Sampling temperature and seed add variability, so they should be fixed across
  comparisons, and each prompt repeated several times if you want to separate
  model quality from sampling luck.
- The model may have seen a public-domain human text during pre-training, and a
  judge who recognises a passage is testing memory, not style. Texts the model is
  unlikely to have memorised are safer, or the risk should be acknowledged.
- The Turing test is a measure of discrimination in a particular setting, not a
  test of intelligence, so passing or failing it should not be claimed to settle
  a philosophical question.
- Writing human texts that imitate the machine can make them unnatural, so the
  pairs should be piloted and any that are obviously written to a formula
  removed.

You should consider how these limitations affect the claims you can reasonably
make from your experiment.

## Expected outcomes and opportunities for evaluation

The expected outcome would be a set of matched human and machine texts, a
judging dataset, and an estimate of how accurately classmates distinguish them.
Importantly, a model that fails the Turing test is not a failed project. A model
that is easy to detect, and a clear account of which tells gave it away, is a
strong result.

You could evaluate the overall detection rate, the detection rate for each
quantisation and temperature, and the reasons judges gave. You could compare the
confusion matrix for human and machine items, and ask whether judges are better
at spotting 3-bit than 8-bit text. You could also compare the vocabulary and
repetition of human and machine texts, and connect those measurements to the
reasons judges gave.

You could pick three pairs that were judged incorrectly and read them closely.
You could ask what made the human text look machine-like, or the machine text
look human. If you can answer that, you have a stronger evaluation than a single
detection rate. You could finish by considering what the experiment says about
the Turing test as a measure: whether it tests intelligence, imitation, or the
judge's expectations, and what would change if the judges were experts, the
texts were longer, or the machine were allowed to revise its output. You could
discuss those questions in your evaluation.

## Suitability as an EPQ artefact

This could make a strong EPQ artefact because designing the test requires you
to make a series of decisions that you can research, justify and evaluate. The
generated texts and judging sheet provide a tangible outcome, while the
experiment provides evidence of planning, research, problem-solving and
reflection. The no-code route leaves you time for experimental design,
participant management and interpretation.

Crucially, the project is open-ended. There is no single correct genre,
quantisation, temperature or judging design, and the quality of your EPQ does
not depend simply on making the machine pass. You have scope to make and
justify your own decisions about the prompts, the human examples and the
judging design.

You could also discuss questions from philosophy and psychology: what it means
to sound human, how people use cues to judge authenticity, and whether a test of
discrimination can tell you anything about intelligence. You could use your judges' reasons as evidence in that discussion.
